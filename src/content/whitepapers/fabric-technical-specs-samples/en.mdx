## The Shortcut That Costs More Than You Save

I've lost count of how many times I've heard this phrase: "Send me a price and if it's good, we'll go." No sample. No technical data sheet. No specification of gsm or composition. Just price.

I understand it. Time is pressing, the collection has a deadline, the budget is tight. But that shortcut has a cost that almost always exceeds the time you were trying to save.

This article is about why fabric samples and technical data sheets are not bureaucracy: they are the only real way to control what you'll receive in production.

## Fabric Is Not a Commodity

In workwear, there's a tendency to treat fabric as if it were a standard ingredient that comes the same from any source. A 65/35 polyester-cotton is a 65/35, right? In theory, yes. In practice, two fabrics with exactly the same declared composition can behave completely differently in washing, in use, in embroidery, in colour durability.

The differences lie in the yarn type — carded or combed, twist, count — in the fabric construction — warp and weft density, weave type — and in finishing processes — mercerising, softeners, anti-pilling. None of those details appear in the composition percentage.

A physical sample tells you what no percentage can: how the fabric feels, how it drapes, how it reacts to friction, whether the colour has real depth or looks flat, whether the finish has the handle that corresponds to the product's positioning.

## What a Fabric Technical Data Sheet Must Include

A fabric technical data sheet doesn't need to be a twenty-page document, but it does need to answer these questions without ambiguity:

**Exact composition.** Not "approximately 60% cotton". The exact percentage, verified, with declared acceptable tolerance (±3% is standard).

**Gsm.** And if the fabric has finishes that can vary the weight (like softeners), indicate whether the gsm is in greige or finished state.

**Construction.** Jersey, piqué, interlock, woven, twill, poplin. "Knit fabric" is not enough — there are dozens of knit fabrics with completely different behaviours.

**Fabric width.** Relevant for consumption calculation and for detecting possible variations between lots.

**Colour and reference.** Pantone, own reference, or approved physical sample. In workwear with corporate colours, the colour reference is as important as the composition.

**Required fastness tests.** Wash, light, rub fastness. If the product is going to comply with a specific standard — ISO 20471, EN 13688, OEKO-TEX — those tests need to be on the data sheet.

**Intended care instructions.** What will appear on the composition and washing label. It has to match what the actual fabric can withstand.

## Why Gsm Matters More Than It Seems

In many European workwear brands, the gsm discussion reduces to a cost negotiation: less weight, less cost. And there's some truth to that. But the relationship between gsm and product behaviour is much more complex.

A 200 g/m² polo and a 240 g/m² polo in the same composition are not the same product at different weights: they are two products with different use profiles. The 200g one performs better in summer and in high physical activity environments. The 240g one has more body, more presence, more durability in industrial washes. Presenting them as equivalent options at different prices is a product positioning error.

The same applies to t-shirts, work trousers, softshell jackets. Gsm is not just cost — it's technical specification with direct impact on product service life and on end user quality perception.

## The Sample Process: How It Should Work

In a well-structured product management, the fabric sample process has several phases:

**Reference sample:** the fabric the supplier proposes as a starting point. It tells you which direction they're thinking. It may be far from what you need or it may be exactly what you're looking for.

**Development sample:** if the reference needs adjustments — in gsm, composition, colour or finish — this is the sample with the changes incorporated. There may be one or several rounds.

**Pre-production sample (PP sample):** the fabric with the exact composition, gsm, colour and finishes of production, before the full lot is launched. This is the sample you officially approve. From here, production starts.

**Production sample (TOP or Shipment sample):** a sample taken from the actual production lot, to verify that what's been made matches what was approved.

Skipping any of these steps — especially the PP sample — means assuming a risk that can materialise as several thousand units of product that don't match what you expected.

## What Happens Without a Technical Data Sheet

Without a technical data sheet, the factory produces what it interprets you need. And the factory, when it interprets, tends to optimise toward its own interests: the most available fabric, the easiest to work with, what they have in stock. It may coincide with what you wanted. It may not.

Without an approved sample, you have no basis for a claim if the production product isn't what you expected. Legally and operationally, you're in no-man's-land.

Technical data sheets and samples are not paperwork. They are the real contract between what you imagine and what you receive.
